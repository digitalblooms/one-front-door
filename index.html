Phoenix Court Works Workshop No. 04 · April 2026

One Front Door

A wellbeing signposter for the neighbourhood, built across one workshop session using Granola, ChatGPT and Claude.

Phoenix Court, London NW1 1EY ~1 hour workshop 1 prototype, 4 artefacts

The brief was to use AI to make something genuinely useful for this neighbourhood. The breakout surfaced three pains — surplus food logistics, digital exclusion, and a fragmented mental health signposting landscape with six-week waits. We picked the wellbeing one and built it. Below is everything the workshop produced, in roughly the order it was made.

01 What the room said

The breakout, by theme

The session was attended by members of local communities. Below is the conversation grouped into the patterns that actually shaped what got built — not in chronological order, but in roughly the order the ideas earned their place.

i. Pain  01

Surplus food, last-mile logistics

A local food pantry operates one day a week. Anything unsold and not lasting until the next week gets broadcast through WhatsApp — the call goes out for whoever can grab leftover potatoes or carrots. The bottleneck isn't goodwill, it's logistics: matching food to drivers, in time, at scale.

ii. Pain  02

Digital exclusion, especially around language

Most council and benefits services have moved online, which filters out elderly residents and people who don't speak English fluently. The Afghan community was named specifically. Across one organisation, staff collectively speak 23 languages — but resources are stretched, and you can't have everyone on shift at once. Translators and family advocates are in short supply.

iii. Pain  03

Mental health and the six-week wait

The waiting list for 1-to-1 mental health support runs at six weeks. One organisation has two part-time staff doing visits and demand is rising. Walk-in services and workshops exist; signposting between organisations does happen — but it depends on individual workers happening to know who's offering what this week.

"The waiting lists are quite high. We have a walk-in service and workshops, but getting that one-to-one or even signposting working is hard."

iv. Tension

The instinct to build a platform — and the suspicion of it

Early ideas leaned toward a Camden-wide marketplace: residents and orgs both posting needs and offers, gamified contributions, badges for hours volunteered. But the room kept catching itself. "Once it's, okay, let's build this platform — I already don't want to join it because it's another thing that will spend a lot of information at me."

Open consumer platforms also raise real safeguarding concerns for residents who are already isolated and vulnerable. Someone offering to come round and cook isn't the same thing as a vetted volunteer. Whoever builds this has to take that seriously.

v. Reframe

Slow time, not fast tools

A counter-current ran through the discussion: instead of chasing speed, what if AI helped Camden slow down? Reframe a food delivery as a slow walk. Reframe a lonely meal as company. Use AI in the background to surface these moments through interfaces residents already use — WhatsApp, voice — rather than adding another app to learn.

"The interface should be agnostic and AI should be in the background. If they know how to use WhatsApp, surface it through WhatsApp."

vi. The shift

From "what should AI do?" to "what admin can we strip out?"

The clearest moment in the breakout: someone naming that you can't have an AI football coach, you can't have an AI sit with someone. The human services have to stay human. But AI can absolutely take the translation, the form-filling, the calendar lookups, the directory-trawling off the humans doing the parts only humans can do.

"What we're trying to create here is connecting people. The services will remain — you cannot get AI to move food or give psychological services. But we can get AI to help knowing that there is a therapist who's taken a sabbatical and has some time."

What the room agreed on by the end

  • The interface should sit inside WhatsApp (or whatever residents already use), not a new app
  • The AI's job is matching and admin, not delivering the service itself
  • The strongest version is B2B-light — a flow that connects orgs to each other so a resident on one's six-week list can reach another's spare slot this week
  • Whatever gets built should be scoped narrow enough to test in weeks, not months

03 Why this won

Three solutions, one picked

The breakout surfaced three distinct pains, and the instinct was to build something for each. By the end of the session we had three sketches. Picking one to actually build wasn't obvious — they all addressed real needs, all played to AI's genuine strengths, all could ship in weeks rather than months. What follows is how we chose.

The shorthand: each candidate was scored on five things — depth of pain, breadth of reach, what AI uniquely unlocks, safeguarding load, and how testable it is in four weeks. The third option won on three of the five. The other two stay alive as future builds, and the matching engine underneath all three is structurally the same, so building one well makes the others cheaper.

i.

Pantry Relay

Shelved for now

A WhatsApp-first surplus-food matcher. A pantry coordinator messages a bot — "40 hot meals, ready 6pm, need collected, can split" — and the AI parses it into structured fields, alerts pre-vetted drivers whose stated availability matches, and reports back to the coordinator as portions get claimed.

The pain is real, the AI's job is unambiguous (parse + match + notify), and Cook for Good were in the room ready to pilot. It nearly won on testability alone.

Why not now Narrow user base by design — a handful of pantry coordinators and drivers. Strong as a pilot, but the workshop's ambition was to demonstrate something with broad neighbourhood relevance. Pantry Relay solves a logistics problem; the room's deeper concern was about residents who can't reach services at all.
ii.

23 Languages

Shelved for now

A multilingual form-and-letter helper. A community champion photographs an English letter or form. The AI translates it, explains what it actually means in plain terms, flags deadlines, and drafts a reply or fills the form via a short voice conversation in the resident's language. The champion reviews everything before submission.

This was the closest runner-up. The pain is acute, AI translation is genuinely strong, and the "trusted human reviews before send" pattern answers the safeguarding question cleanly.

Why not now High safeguarding load. Form-filling and benefit letters are exactly the situations where mistakes have material consequences — wrong field, missed deadline, suspended claim. Doing this responsibly needs a careful pilot with caseworker oversight, which is more setup than a four-week test allows. Worth doing properly later, not quickly now.
iii.

One Front Door

Chosen

A wellbeing signposter inside WhatsApp. A resident describes how they're feeling, in any language. The AI runs a 30-second triage — language, kind of help, when — and matches them to a specific service slot available this week. It doesn't diagnose. It signposts.

The win condition wasn't ambition. It was that this candidate solved across all three pains at once: it's a directory tool (mental health), a language tool (multilingual entry), and a friction-reducer (the six-week wait gets shorter when peer support, drop-ins, and group activities are surfaced alongside 1-to-1).

Why this Broadest reach with the lowest safeguarding ceiling. Signposting to existing, vetted services is a fundamentally lower-risk operation than form-filling or food delivery — the AI is matching to options the orgs already approve. And the matching engine, once built, generalises. Pantry Relay and 23 Languages would use the same core. Build this first, the others get cheaper.

The other thing the matrix flatters into clarity: One Front Door is the only candidate where you could imagine the same engine, lightly reskinned, doing the other two jobs later. A signposter that matches residents to wellbeing slots is a matching engine plus a wellbeing-shaped data set. Swap the data set for "surplus food and drivers" and you have Pantry Relay. Swap it for "letters that need explaining," and you're closer to 23 Languages than you'd think. The pick wasn't just about today; it was about what gets unlocked next.

04 How the prototype was designed

Every choice in the mockup, and why

The WhatsApp prototype looks simple, but each surface decision is doing work. What follows is the design rationale, choice by choice. If you fork this, these are the decisions you'd want to revisit in your own context — they're not obvious, and a few are deliberately controversial.

Decision · 01

WhatsApp, not a custom app

Considered A bespoke web app or PWA, branded for the neighbourhood, indexed in app stores
→
Picked WhatsApp Business API, surfaced as a verified service account

Why. Adoption is the only metric that matters for a signposter. Every new app installed is a barrier; every familiar interface is a bridge. The room kept returning to a line that became the design's north star: "the interface should be agnostic and AI should be in the background." WhatsApp is already on the phones of the elderly residents, the non-English-speaking residents, and the parents the service most needs to reach. A custom app filters out exactly the people the brief is for.

Decision · 02

The triage is two questions, not seven

Considered A thorough intake form covering symptoms, history, severity, location, language, age, support network
→
Picked Two clarifying questions max, from a pool the AI selects from contextually

Why. The mockup asks "one-to-one or around people?" and "this week or next?" — that's it. A longer triage is more accurate but exponentially less likely to be completed by someone in distress. The cost of a slightly worse match is one missed session; the cost of an abandoned conversation is the entire person walking away. Designing for completion rate over precision is the right trade in this domain.

Decision · 03

Three options, not ten

Considered A scrollable list of every available service that matches, ranked by relevance
→
Picked Exactly three concrete options, each with a real day, time, and one-line description

Why. Decision paralysis is the second-largest barrier to taking up support, after access itself. Three is the upper limit at which a person under load can hold options in their head and choose. More than three turns into a directory — which is what the room said already exists, and isn't being used. The list is meant to feel like a friend texting you suggestions, not like searching a database.

Decision · 04

The Samaritans line lives in every list, quietly

Considered A separate "are you in crisis?" flow triggered only by detected risk language
→
Picked A persistent, low-key Samaritans footer on every options message, regardless of triage path

Why. Risk detection is fragile. Someone can be in crisis without using the words a classifier expects, and the consequences of a missed detection are severe. Persistent visibility — softly worded, not alarming — costs nothing for the 99 out of 100 conversations that aren't crises, and is potentially life-relevant for the one that is. The decision was inspired by the way good barbers and good GPs operate: the door's always open, you don't have to perform distress to be allowed through it.

Decision · 05

Aisha is named, the booking is confirmed

Considered A generic "see you Tuesday at 2pm" confirmation
→
Picked A specific volunteer's name, with a relevant detail about them ("speaks Dari and Arabic too")

Why. This is the moment the prototype most needs to do work. A person who has just told a stranger they've been crying for two weeks needs to be handed off to another person, not into another transaction. Naming Aisha turns the destination from "a service" into "someone." The language detail isn't decoration — it's a quiet signal that the system noticed who the resident is, and matched accordingly. This is where the human-in-the-loop principle stops being abstract.

Decision · 06

Verified service account badge, "Service account" status

Considered Disguising the AI as a person, or making it feel like a friendly mascot
→
Picked Clearly labelled as a verified service, tone warm but unambiguously not human

Why. Honesty about what's on the other end of the conversation is non-negotiable in a wellbeing context. A resident reaching out at low ebb deserves to know whether they're talking to a person or a routing system. The verification badge doubles as a safeguarding signal — it's how you tell the legitimate council-backed service from the scam impersonator that will inevitably appear within months of launch. The voice is warm because warmth is appropriate; the identity is clear because pretending otherwise erodes trust the moment it's noticed.

Decision · 07

Booking confirmation is opt-in for reminders

Considered Auto-enrolling in reminders, calendar invites, follow-up check-ins by default
→
Picked A single, optional "want me to send a reminder Tuesday morning?" with a clean no-thanks

Why. Notification overload is its own form of harm — for residents managing anxiety, an unexpected ping is sometimes worse than a missed session. The default is silence; the resident chooses how much contact they want. This also models the right consent pattern for the service overall: ask before doing, every time.

What was deliberately cut from the prototype: any progress tracking ("you've used this service three times"), any account creation, any feedback form after a session, any social or community features, any gamification of usage. The shape that emerged was a service that does one thing and gets out of the way — closer to dialling 111 than to opening an app.

05 The shape of the process

What the AI actually did

The workshop framing assigned a role to each tool — Granola to capture, ChatGPT to build, Claude to refine. In practice the lines blurred, but the division of labour held up well.

Granola recorded the breakout and produced a transcript clean enough that the three pains could be pulled directly from it without re-listening. It didn't replace note-taking; it removed the friction that usually means notes never get written up. Worth a caveat: Granola's a great tool, but it struggles in larger group conversations where people talk over each other — which we did. Useful for the shape of the discussion, less reliable for exact attribution.

Claude did most of the synthesis and production work — drafting the three-solutions document, writing the one-page brief, generating the WhatsApp mockup as working HTML, and packaging this site. The pattern that worked was: feed it the messy human input, ask for a specific output, push back when it produced something generic.

The bottleneck isn't a shortage of services. It's the friction between a person in distress and the right service for them, this week, in their language. — from the one-page brief

The clearest principle to come out of the breakout — and the one the final solution leans on hardest — is that AI's job here is admin and matching, not delivery. You can't have an AI football coach. You can't have an AI sit with someone struggling. But you can have an AI take the translation, the form-filling, the calendar lookups, the directory-trawling off the humans who do the parts only humans can do.

06 The mockup, in situ

What the resident actually sees

A single conversational entry point — over WhatsApp, voice note, or text — in any language. The AI asks two or three clarifying questions and matches the resident to a specific slot available this week. It books it if the org has shared its calendar; otherwise it gives the right phone number and what to ask for. No new app to learn.
